# M3.2 Gameplay balance — report

**Status:** done; awaiting PM review. **Human play-testing of the new rules is still pending**
(see §9). Every number here comes from seeded AI-vs-AI simulations, not from people playing.

*M3.4-A update (historical report):* the castle victory and the fixed castles measured here were
replaced in M3.4-A by elimination-only victory and deployable cover blocks. Cover Guard 50 % and
Repair 150 were kept, but these win rates and match lengths describe the old rules only. The
benchmark script was removed from the working tree; it and this report reproduce at commit
`86b90fa` (`git show 86b90fa:scripts/balance_benchmark.py`).

## Summary

| | Before M3.2 | After M3.2 |
|---|---|---|
| Defend / Rally | halves damage to the defending characters | the same, **plus Castle Guard:** enemy shots do half damage to that team's castle blocks (rounded down) until the team's next turn |
| Engineer Repair | up to 75 castle HP, cooldown 4 | **up to 150 castle HP**, cooldown 4 (no revived blocks, no block above 100 HP) |
| Castle victory | 60 % destroyed | **unchanged** (60 %) |

Why (Normal / Hard; paired comparisons on identical seeds, 95 % margins of error):
- **Castle Guard makes Defend matter for the castle.** A defensive action now prevents castle
  damage 84–92 % of the time (about 50 HP each time), against 6–11 % of defensive actions that
  prevented *any* damage before. A defend-when-threatened strategy wins **+13 ± 6** points more
  often on Normal and **+4 ± 3** on Hard. Matches get 0.17 rounds longer on Normal and no longer
  on Hard. Defend is still not a free win: when the castle is threatened, attacking remains the
  better move on average (−11 to −12 points for defending instead, was −20 to −21).
- **Repair 150 makes Repair a real choice.** At the moment a Repair is possible, repairing instead
  of attacking now scores −2 ± 10 (Normal) and −3 ± 10 (Hard) points, statistically even with
  attacking; it was −5 ± 9 and **−22 ± 8**. A repair-focused strategy wins **+23 ± 10** /
  **+9 ± 7** points more often.
- **Together** they add 0.85 rounds on Normal and 0.32 on Hard. No match stalled, and nothing
  else measurable moved: the shipped AI's results, first-move advantage and how matches end are
  the same within noise.
- **Easy agrees** (a smaller check of four variants, §5). With both changes, every strategy that
  defends or repairs does better: the shipped AI +15 ± 9, defensive +12 ± 9, repair-aware
  +29 ± 15 points. Easy matches get the longest: +1.39 rounds, to 9–12 rounds on average.
- **60 % stays.** 70 % made matches about one round longer and gave no consistent benefit.

## 1. Goal and limits

The brief: make Defend and Engineer Repair strategically useful without redesigning the game.
Allowed changes were Castle Guard, a Repair amount of 75–150 and a castle threshold of 60–70 %,
each only with convincing evidence and a small implementation. Not allowed: new attack
mechanics, counterattacks, extra actions per turn, rebuilt blocks. One action per team turn,
Defend's 50 % character damage reduction, the three classes and their stats, the three AI
difficulties, Player vs AI, local PvP, 1v1–3v3 and the projectile physics are unchanged.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 The benchmark

`scripts/balance_benchmark.py` plays seeded AI-vs-AI matches. It never edits a project file: each
worker process sets the variant's three rule numbers in memory (`battle.CASTLE_GUARD_PERCENT`,
`Engineer.REPAIR_POOL`, `castle.LOSS_PERCENT`) and then uses the real `Battle` and the real
`AIController`. The generated tables are in `docs/M3.2_benchmark_results.md` (Normal and Hard)
and `docs/M3.2_benchmark_results_easy.md` (Easy).

**Rule variants:**

| Variant | Castle Guard | Repair | Castle loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — baseline (the M3.1 rules) | no | 75 | 60 % |
| B — Castle Guard | yes | 75 | 60 % |
| C125 / C150 — bigger Repair | no | 125 / 150 | 60 % |
| D125 / D150 — both | yes | 125 / 150 | 60 % |
| E70 — higher threshold | no | 75 | 70 % |
| ELIM — elimination only (fallback) | no | 75 | off |

Castle Guard was first written as a subclass inside the benchmark. After the decision it was
moved into `battle.py`, and 300 randomly chosen recorded matches, replayed with the production
code, gave exactly the same results.

**Strategies** (all use the real AI's aiming, so only the decision differs):
- *aggressive*: attacks every turn (Power Shot still used); never Defends, Rallies or Repairs.
- *existing*: the shipped M3.1 AI.
- *defensive*: Defends or Rallies whenever its castle could fall to one strong hit (≤ 300 HP
  before falling, a Power Shot on four blocks) or a character is below half HP, never two turns
  in a row; otherwise plays like the shipped AI.
- *repair-aware*: Repairs whenever its Engineer is ready and the surviving blocks are missing at
  least one full Repair; otherwise plays like the shipped AI.

**Fairness controls:**
- Mirrored rosters only (both teams have the same roles in the same slots). Unequal teams were
  never compared.
- The tested strategy moves first in exactly half of its matches.
- Common random numbers: match *i* uses seeds 1000·*i* + 1 (left) and 1000·*i* + 2 (right) in
  every variant, so two variants can be compared match by match ("paired").
- A 40-round cap; a match still going is "unfinished" and scores ½.

**The grid (per difficulty and variant):** 1v1, 2v2 and 3v3 with 48 matches per size for each
pairing — existing, defensive and repair-aware against aggressive, plus aggressive and existing
mirror matches. The repair-aware strategy needs an Engineer, so it has 96 matches instead of 144.
Normal and Hard ran all 8 variants (10,560 matches). Easy ran A, B, C150 and D150 with 24
matches per size (see §5).

### 2.2 Decision points

Whole-match win rates mix good and bad uses of an action. So the benchmark also measures single
decisions. It plays a seeded existing-AI mirror match until the side to move reaches a decision
point, copies the whole match, and plays both choices to the end with the same AIs and the same
random state:
- **defend_castle:** the castle could fall to one strong hit; Defend / Rally vs attack.
- **defend_hurt:** a character is below half HP; Defend / Rally vs attack.
- **repair:** an Engineer is ready and the castle is missing at least one full Repair; Repair vs attack.

*Value of acting* = win rate after acting − win rate after attacking. Positive means the
defensive action was the better move.

### 2.3 Completed experiments and exact sample counts

| Difficulty | Variants | Matches (strategy grid) | Decision samples: attempted → completed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | all 8 | 5,280: 672 per variant (ELIM 576; it has no repair-aware pairing) | 3,168 → 2,288 |
| Hard | all 8 | 5,280: 672 per variant (ELIM 576) | 3,168 → 2,008 |
| Easy | A, B, C150, D150 only | 1,344: 336 per variant | 864 → 696 |
| **Total** | | **11,904** | **7,200 → 4,992** |

A decision sample is *attempted* for every seed. It is *completed* only if that match reached
the decision point before it ended; the tables count completed samples only. For example, a
match can end before any character drops below half HP. Normal / Hard completed samples by
decision:
- defend_castle 981 / 774;
- defend_hurt 299 / 374;
- repair 1,008 / 860.

Nothing else was run as part of the final results. C125, D125, E70 and ELIM were **not** run on
Easy.

**Reproducing the results.** The tables come from the committed script with these commands, run
from the project folder (Python 3.11.9, 17 worker processes; the number of workers doesn't change
the results):

```
python scripts/balance_benchmark.py
python scripts/balance_benchmark.py --difficulties easy --variants A,B,C150,D150 --decision-variants A,B,C150,D150 --matches 24 --samples 24 --out docs/M3.2_benchmark_results_easy.md
```

Seeds are fixed:
- the *i*-th match (*i* = 0, 1, 2, …) of each roster, pairing, variant and difficulty uses
  `AIController` seeds 1000·*i* + 1 (left) and 1000·*i* + 2 (right). The same *i* therefore gets
  the same seeds in every variant, which is what makes the comparisons paired.
- Decision sample *i* of each roster uses the same two seeds.

Each match runs on its own, so the results don't depend on the order in which workers finish.
The Normal and Hard tables were produced twice: once with the first, benchmark-only Castle Guard
and once with the production code. Every match and every decision sample came out identical
(§11).

### 2.4 Statistics

- Win rate = wins + ½ × (draws and unfinished). The margin of error is the 95 % normal
  approximation, 1.96 · √(p(1 − p)/n). With n = 144 that is about ±8 points; with n = 48 (one
  team size) about ±14.
- Variant comparisons are paired: the margin comes from the spread of the per-match
  differences, which is much smaller because most matches end the same way under both rules.
- About 40 comparisons were made, so one or two may look "significant" by chance. No decision
  rests on a single number: each adopted change is supported by several measures that agree.

What is different on Easy:
- **Aiming.** Easy aims badly, so the aggressive strategy wastes many shots. Defending and
  repairing are already about even with attacking under the old rules.
- **The shipped AI benefits most here.** It wins 65 % against pure aggression with D150, up from
  50 %, because it defends more often on Easy (10 % of its actions).
- **Match length grows most on Easy.** With D150, existing-AI mirror matches average
  9.2 / 11.0 / 12.3 rounds (1v1 / 2v2 / 3v3), against 8.0 / 9.6 / 10.7 under the old rules. That
  is +1.39 rounds; the longest match was 24 rounds, and none reached the 40-round cap.
- **First move.** The first mover won 60 % ± 8 of mirror matches with D150 and 67 % ± 8 with the
  old rules (n = 144 each).
- **Defensive actions.** 81 % of them prevented damage with D150, against 19 % before.
- **One cell runs against the decision.** On Easy, Castle Guard alone (B) makes defending a hurt
  character look worse than before: −21 ± 14 against +0 ± 17. It has only 29–33 samples, and the
  margins overlap; D150 reads −6 ± 18. It is reported here rather than ignored, and it is a
  reason to watch defending at low HP in the play-tests.

## 6. Answers to the brief's questions

1. **Is Defend useful when the castle is threatened?** Before M3.2, hardly: it protected only
   the defender, so 89–94 % of defensive actions prevented nothing. With Castle Guard, 84–92 % of
   them prevent castle damage (about 50 HP each), and 17–30 % save the castle or a character.
   Attacking is still usually better when the castle is threatened (−10 to −16 points). That is
   built into one action per turn: a guard saves about half of one enemy shot, but the team gives
   up its own shot. Defend now pays off when the enemy's next shot matters more than your own,
   rather than almost never.
2. **Does Repair give meaningful survival value?** At 75 HP it was a mistake on Hard (−22 ± 8
   points). At 150 HP it is even with attacking at both difficulties (−2 ± 10, −3 ± 10), and a
   repair-focused team wins about twice as often against pure aggression on Normal (22 % → 45 %).
3. **Does the combined rule make matches too long?** No: +0.85 rounds on Normal (about +13 %),
   +0.32 on Hard and +1.39 on Easy (about +15 %). Normal and Hard matches are still *shorter*
   than the design's 8–15-round target (R2); Easy matches now average 9–12 rounds, inside it.
4. **Is first-move advantage unfair?** It is measurable: the side moving first wins 59–66 % of
   mirror matches between equal AIs on Normal and Hard (n = 288 per cell), and 60–67 % on Easy
   (n = 144). No approved variant removes it (E70: 58 % on Normal but 63 % on Hard). In Player
   vs AI the human always moves first, so the advantage helps the player. Not fixed in M3.2;
   see §10.
5. **Is elimination more common?** No change: 5 of 144 Normal matches and 32 of 144 Hard matches
   end by elimination with D150 (A: 7 and 32). The castle stays the main way to win while castle
   loss is on.
6. **Are the AI's actions still legal and sensible?** In all 11,904 matches (Easy, Normal and
   Hard): 0 refused actions, 0 safety-net Defends. The AI needed no change: it judges every option on a copy of the battle
   with the real rules, so it already "knows" Castle Guard and Repair 150. It repairs more often
   now and its results against the aggressive strategy are unchanged. It was *not* made to defend
   more when its castle is threatened, because the decision-point results say that would make it
   worse.
7. **Does any strategy stall?** No. Nothing reached the 40-round cap. The longest existing-AI
   mirror match with the new rules was 17 rounds on Normal, 10 on Hard and 24 on Easy. One 150-HP Repair every 4 rounds can't keep up with the 3–4 enemy shots of the same
   period (about 100–200 HP each).

## 7. Decision

**Adopted: Castle Guard + Repair 150 (variant D150), with the 60 % threshold kept.**
- **Castle Guard:** the effect is direct, it shows up at all three difficulties, it costs little
  match length, and Defend still doesn't dominate. The implementation is about fifteen lines,
  comments included.
- **Repair 150 rather than 125:** the two can't be told apart statistically (150 vs 125: +5 ± 8 /
  +4 ± 6 points). But 150 is the only value that is clearly better than 75 at *both*
  difficulties, and 150 HP is about one typical cannon hit (2–3 blocks), which is easy to
  explain. It costs about 0.3 rounds more than 125. If play-testers find Repair too strong, 125
  is the fallback: one constant.
- **60 % kept:** 70 % lengthens matches by about a round. Its effects point in different
  directions on Normal and Hard, and the brief prefers 60 %.

The deliberately simple defensive and repair-aware strategies still lose to pure aggression,
even with the new rules. That is intended: defending or repairing *every* time gives up too many
shots. The rules make these actions sensible at the right moment, not mandatory.

## 10. Limitations and next experiments

**Limitations:**
- **AI only.** All the evidence comes from AI-vs-AI play. People may defend and repair at
  better or worse moments than the scripted strategies.
- **Sample sizes.** ±14 points for a single team size, about ±8 overall. defend_hurt has only
  20–38 samples per cell when castle loss is on.
- **Fixed seeds.** The results can be reproduced exactly, but they reflect this AI's aiming
  noise.
- **First move.** The first-move advantage (59–67 % in mirror matches) remains.
- **Match length.** Normal and Hard matches remain shorter than the design's 8–15-round target.
  Easy matches, at 9–12 rounds, are inside it.
- **Easy coverage.** Easy was checked for four variants with half the sample size. Its
  defend_hurt cells are small (29–34 samples), and one of them (B) points the other way (§5).
- **Interface.** Castle Guard is shown only as a combat-log line. There is no new on-screen
  marker, by the brief's "no UI visual changes" rule.

**Next experiments (each needs PM approval):**
1. Alternate the side that moves first on each Rematch (or at random), then re-measure
   first-move advantage. This changes the flow of the game, not a combat rule.
2. A short human play-test log: how often people Defend, Rally and Repair, and whether the
   Castle Guard log line is noticed.
3. Only if play-tests show the computer never protects its castle: a threat-aware Defend for the
   AI, measured with the same decision-point method.

## 11. Verification before commit (the PM's interim checks)

1. **Several defenders don't stack.** The guard is a yes/no state ("is any living character
   defending?"), applied once per explosion. In `test_several_defenders_do_not_stack_the_guard`,
   Rally puts three defenders on the field and a 4-block hit does 4 × 25, not less. An injected
   "halve once per defender" defect fails that test and the Rally test.
2. **The guard ends exactly at the intended turn boundary.** The guard is active for every frame
   of the enemy's shot. It ends in `_start_turn` of the defending team, which runs straight after
   that shot lands, before the defending team can act. This is checked for a left and a right
   defender, so the round boundary is covered too (test
   `test_guard_lasts_the_whole_enemy_turn_and_ends_as_the_defender_turn_starts`). Injected
   defects that end the guard when the enemy's turn starts, at the round start, or one turn late
   were all caught.
3. **A defender defeated by the explosion.** The guard is read once, at impact, before any block
   or character is damaged. That explosion's block damage therefore uses the defence state at
   impact, even if the explosion defeats the defender. Nothing can change that state while the
   shot is in the air: no action is possible then. The explosion event records that same value
   as `"guarded"`. Test `test_the_guard_also_covers_the_shot_that_defeats_the_defender`: a 1-HP
   defending Archer is defeated, and the block in the same blast takes 25, not 50. Moving the
   check after the damage fails it.
4. **The M1 cannon duel is unaffected.** The duel has no characters, so `castle_guarded()` is
   always False. I played 60 seeded duels (random aims, each played to the end) with the old
   code (HEAD `b3ba1ac`) and the new code: all 60 games were identical, with the same 9,541
   events, winners, round counts and final block HP. The only difference is that duel explosion
   events now carry `"guarded": False`, a field the duel screen doesn't read. The scripted M1
   duel drive through the real `main.main()` passed 15 / 15 checks and still ends on frame 713,
   as in M1. `test_the_m1_duel_has_no_castle_guard` and the unchanged `tests/test_battle.py`
   cover it in the suite.
5. **Completed experiments and exact sample counts:** §2.3 (Easy is a separate, smaller run of
   four variants).
6. **Reproducibility.** Both result files were written by the committed
   `scripts/balance_benchmark.py`, which has not changed since those runs started. The commands,
   seeds and settings are in §2.3.

   The Normal/Hard run was a second, independent run. The first, before Castle Guard moved into
   `battle.py`, used a benchmark-only subclass. Compared record by record, all 10,560 matches and
